So you're thinking about buying your first home. That's exciting! It's also completely normal to feel a little lost about where to start.

The good news? The mortgage side follows the same basic path every time. Whether you've got your eye on a condo downtown or a starter home in one of Calgary's newer communities, here's what to expect, one step at a time.

## Step 1: Take a look at your credit

Your credit report is one of the first things a lender checks. It shows how you've handled things like credit cards, car loans, and phone bills. It plays a big part in whether you're approved and what terms you're offered.

That's why it's worth checking early. If there's a mistake, or a card that's close to its limit, you'll have time to sort it out before you apply. A few months of prep can make a real difference.

Honestly, this is where we spend a lot of our time with clients. We don't just understand mortgages; we understand credit. If you're not sure where yours stands, that's a great place to start the conversation.

## Step 2: Figure out your down payment

Your down payment is the part of the price you pay yourself. Your mortgage covers the rest. The minimum depends on the price of the home:

| Purchase price | Minimum down payment |
| --- | --- |
| $500,000 or less | 5% of the price |
| $500,000 to $1.5 million | 5% of the first $500,000, plus 10% of anything above that |
| $1.5 million or more | 20% of the price |

Here's a quick example (for illustration only). On a $550,000 home, you'd need 5% of $500,000 ($25,000) plus 10% of the remaining $50,000 ($5,000). That's $30,000 in total.

One heads-up: if you're self-employed or working on your credit, a lender may ask for more than the minimum.

### Two programs worth knowing about

- **First Home Savings Account (FHSA):** lets first-time buyers save for a home tax-free. You can put in $8,000 in the year you open your first one, up to $40,000 in total.
- **Home Buyers' Plan (HBP):** lets first-time buyers take up to $60,000 out of their RRSP for a home, then pay it back over time.

Both come with eligibility rules and tax details, so check with your bank or tax professional before you use them.

## Step 3: Get to know mortgage default insurance

Putting down less than 20%? Then you'll usually need mortgage default insurance. You might hear it called "CMHC insurance." It comes from CMHC, Sagen, or Canada Guaranty.

Here's the part that surprises people: this insurance protects the lender, not you. What it does for you is let you buy with a smaller down payment.

The cost is a percentage of your mortgage. The smaller your down payment, the higher that percentage. You can pay it up front or add it to your mortgage (in which case you'll pay interest on it too).

## Step 4: Understand the stress test

To qualify, you'll need to show you could still afford your payments if your interest rate were higher than the one you're actually getting. That's the mortgage stress test.

Whether your mortgage is insured or uninsured, you're tested at your rate plus 2%, or at 5.25%, whichever is higher. If you're putting down less than 20%, the mortgage insurer also has its own approval requirements. Lenders that aren't federally regulated, like Alberta credit unions, don't fall under the federal rule directly, though many use a similar test.

Yes, the stress test usually lowers how much you can borrow. That can feel frustrating. But it also gives you some breathing room if rates go up or life changes.

## Step 5: Choose your amortization and term

These two sound alike, but they mean different things:

- **Amortization** is how long it would take to pay off the whole mortgage. Since December 15, 2024, first-time buyers can choose up to 30 years on an insured mortgage. A longer amortization means lower monthly payments, but more interest over time.
- **Term** is how long your current rate and conditions last, often one to five years. When it ends, you renew. That's a great time to look at your options again.

You'll also pick a fixed or variable rate. Neither is right for everyone. The better fit depends on your budget, how comfortable you are with change, and your plans.

## Step 6: Get pre-approved

A pre-approval is when a lender looks at your income, debts, credit, and down payment before you start house hunting. It gives you a realistic price range, and it can hold an interest rate for a set period.

Just keep in mind that a pre-approval isn't a final approval. Once your offer is accepted, the lender still reviews the home itself and double-checks your paperwork. So until you have your keys, try to keep things steady: no new loans, no big purchases on credit, and no job changes if you can help it.

## Step 7: Plan for closing costs

Your down payment isn't the only money you'll need on closing day. In Calgary, common costs include:

- Legal fees for your lawyer
- A home inspection
- Alberta Land Titles fees to register the home and your mortgage
- Property tax and utility adjustments
- Title insurance, if your lawyer or lender requires it

The nice thing about buying here is that Alberta doesn't charge a land transfer tax like Ontario or B.C. do. Still, it's smart to set some money aside for these costs on top of your down payment.

## Step 8: From "offer accepted" to getting your keys

Here's what usually happens next:

1. You send your documents to the lender, like pay stubs, a job letter, and bank statements.
2. The lender approves the home and sends you a mortgage commitment.
3. You remove your financing condition by the date in your contract.
4. You meet with your lawyer to sign the mortgage paperwork.
5. On possession day, the money changes hands and you get your keys!

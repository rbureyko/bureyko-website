If you've started looking into mortgages, you've probably heard about "the stress test." It sounds a bit intimidating, doesn't it? Don't worry. It's simpler than it sounds, and once you understand it, you'll know exactly why your numbers look the way they do.

## What is the stress test?

The stress test is a check lenders use to make sure you could still afford your mortgage if interest rates went up.

Instead of qualifying you at the rate you'll actually pay, the lender qualifies you at a higher rate. If your budget still works at that higher rate, you pass.

## How the qualifying rate is set

The qualifying rate works the same way whether your mortgage is insured (less than 20% down) or uninsured (20% or more down). You're tested at whichever is higher:

- your mortgage rate plus 2%, or
- 5.25%

Here's a quick example, for illustration only. If you were offered a rate of 4.5%, you'd be tested at 6.5%. If you were offered 3%, you'd be tested at 5.25%, since that's the higher of the two.

If you're putting down less than 20%, there are a few extra steps on top of the stress test. Your mortgage also needs default insurance, and the mortgage insurer has its own approval requirements. But the qualifying rate itself is the same.

One important thing: **you don't actually pay the higher rate.** Your payments are based on your real mortgage rate. The stress test rate is only used to check whether you qualify.

## Who has to pass it?

The federal rule applies to federally regulated lenders, like the big banks. Lenders that aren't federally regulated, such as Alberta credit unions, aren't directly covered by it, though many use a similar test.

## How it affects what you can borrow

Because you're qualified at a higher rate, the stress test usually lowers the maximum amount a lender will approve. That can be frustrating, especially when you know you can handle the real payments.

But there's a silver lining. It builds in some breathing room, so if rates rise or money gets tighter, you're less likely to feel squeezed.

## Do you have to pass it again at renewal?

Good news here.

If you renew with your current lender, you generally won't be stress tested again. And since November 21, 2024, if you have an uninsured mortgage and switch to a new federally regulated lender at renewal, you're not expected to pass the stress test again either. That applies as long as you're not borrowing more or stretching out your amortization.

Insured mortgage holders can also switch lenders at renewal without re-qualifying under the stress test.

## Ways to make the numbers work

If the stress test is limiting your budget, a few things may help:

- **A larger down payment** means a smaller mortgage to qualify for.
- **Paying down other debts**, like a car loan or credit card, frees up room in your budget.
- **A longer amortization**, where you qualify, can lower your payments. First-time buyers and buyers of new builds can choose up to 30 years on an insured mortgage.
- **Adding a co-borrower** with steady income can strengthen your application.

Every situation is different, and each of these comes with trade-offs. That's where a conversation helps.

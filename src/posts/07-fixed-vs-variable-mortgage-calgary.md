It's one of the most common questions we hear: "Should I go fixed or variable?"

Here's the honest answer: nobody can tell you for sure where rates are headed. So instead of trying to predict the future, it helps to choose the option that fits your life, your budget, and how you sleep at night.

## How a fixed rate works

With a fixed rate, your interest rate stays the same for your whole term. Your payments stay the same too.

That means no surprises. If rates go up, you're protected until your term ends. If rates go down, though, you won't see the benefit until you renew.

Fixed rates are usually a bit higher than variable rates for a similar term. You're paying a little extra for that certainty.

## How a variable rate works

A variable rate can go up or down during your term. It moves with your lender's prime rate. Variable rates are typically a bit lower than fixed rates for a similar term.

Here's the part many people don't realize: there are two kinds of variable mortgages.

- **Adjustable payments.** When the rate changes, your payment changes too. You'll feel rate changes right away in your budget.
- **Fixed payments.** Your payment stays the same, but the split between interest and principal changes. When rates rise, more of each payment goes to interest and less goes to paying down your mortgage.

That second kind can carry more risk than people expect. If rates rise far enough, your whole payment could go to interest, and your balance could actually grow. If you have this type of mortgage and rates are rising, it's a good idea to talk to your lender or broker early.

## Don't forget about penalties

This is the detail that often matters most, and it's easy to overlook.

If you break your mortgage before the term ends (say you sell, move, or refinance), you'll usually pay a penalty. For many fixed-rate mortgages, the penalty is the higher of three months' interest or something called the interest rate differential. That second calculation can add up to a lot, especially with some lenders.

Variable-rate penalties are often closer to three months' interest, but it depends on your contract. Always check how your lender calculates it before you sign.

## Questions to ask yourself

There's no wrong answer to these, but they'll point you in the right direction:

1. **How much room is in my budget?** Could I handle a higher payment if rates went up?
2. **How do I feel about change?** Would watching rates move stress me out?
3. **How stable is my income?** Is it steady, or does it go up and down?
4. **How long will I keep this mortgage?** Is there any chance I'll sell, move, or refinance before the term ends?

If certainty matters most to you, fixed might feel better. If you have some wiggle room and you're comfortable with ups and downs, variable could be worth a look.

## There's also a middle ground

Some lenders offer hybrid mortgages, where part of your mortgage is fixed and part is variable. It's a way to split the difference, though these can be harder to move to another lender later.

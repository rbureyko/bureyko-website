If you've owned your home for a while, you've probably built up some equity. That's the part of your home's value that you own outright: what it's worth, minus what you still owe.

That equity can be a really useful tool. You might use it to renovate, pay off higher-interest debt, or help with a big life expense. But borrowing against your home is a big decision, so let's look at how it works and when it's a good idea.

## What does refinancing mean?

Refinancing means replacing your current mortgage with a new one, usually to borrow more money or change your terms.

It's different from a simple renewal. When you renew, you're carrying on with your existing balance at the end of your term. When you refinance, you're changing the mortgage itself, which usually means applying and qualifying again, including the stress test.

## How much can you access?

With most lenders, you can borrow up to **80% of your home's value** in total, including what you already owe.

Here's an example, for illustration only. If your home is worth $600,000, 80% is $480,000. If you owe $300,000 on your mortgage now, you could potentially access up to $180,000 more, if you qualify.

Your home's value will usually be confirmed with an appraisal.

## Refinance or home equity line of credit?

There are two common ways to use your equity:

- **A refinance** gives you a lump sum, added to your mortgage, which you pay back with your regular mortgage payments. This can work well when you know exactly how much you need, like for debt consolidation.
- **A home equity line of credit (HELOC)** lets you borrow, pay back, and borrow again, up to a limit. You only pay interest on what you use. With federally regulated lenders, the revolving line of credit part can go up to 65% of your home's value. Anything above that, up to 80%, needs to be a regular mortgage that you pay down over time.

A HELOC is flexible, which can be great for things like a renovation done in stages. But that flexibility needs discipline, since it's easy to keep borrowing.

## When refinancing can make sense

- **Consolidating high-interest debt.** Mortgage rates are usually much lower than credit card rates. Rolling that debt into your mortgage can lower your monthly costs. (Just remember, you're now paying it off over a longer time, so it's worth paying it down faster if you can.)
- **Renovations.** Especially ones that improve your home or its value.
- **Big life changes.** Like helping with education costs or other major expenses.
- **At renewal.** If your term is ending anyway, it can be a good time to look at it, since you may avoid a prepayment penalty.

## When to pause and think twice

- **If it means paying a big penalty.** Breaking your mortgage early can be costly. For many fixed-rate mortgages, the penalty is the higher of three months' interest or the interest rate differential. Always get the exact number from your lender first.
- **If the spending would just happen again.** Consolidating debt helps most when the habits that created it have changed too.
- **If the costs outweigh the savings.** There can be legal fees, an appraisal, and in Alberta, a Land Titles fee to register a new mortgage. Add it all up before you decide.

## Let's run the numbers together

Refinancing can be a great move, or not the right one at all, depending on your situation. The best way to know is to compare the full picture: your penalty, the costs, the new rate, and what you'll save.

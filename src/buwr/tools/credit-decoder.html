Cracking the code: payment ratings

Each account has a rating like R1 or I2. The letter is the type of account. The number is how you've paid. R1 and I1 are what you want to see.

RRevolving: credit cards and lines of credit

Negative items like late payments and collections generally stay on your report for about six years, so stop new ones early. Still in school? Student loans usually show up as installment (I) accounts. Once payments start, paying them on time is one of the easiest ways to build strong credit.

What I look at first at the underwriting desk

What lenders checkWhy it matters
Late payments in the last two yearsRecent history counts most. One slip is fixable; a pattern is a red flag.
Card balances compared to limitsCards close to maxed out suggest money is tight, even if you pay on time.
How many accounts, and for how longMany lenders like to see at least two accounts with a couple of years of history.
Collections, even small onesAn unpaid $80 phone bill in collections can hold up an approval.
Recent hard inquiriesLots of applications close together can look like you're short on cash.
Does it match your application?Addresses and debts should line up with what you tell the lender.

Found something wrong? Here's what to do

If you see…Do this
An account you don't recognizeContact the credit bureau right away. It could be a mistake or identity fraud.
A late payment that wasn't lateDispute it with the bureau, and call the lender who reported it.
A debt you paid that still shows a balanceDispute it and send proof of payment.
An old collection that's yoursText Robin before you pay it. How you handle it matters, and you'll want the result in writing.
The same account listed twiceDispute the duplicate with the bureau.

How to dispute: do it online with each bureau separately (fixing one doesn't fix the other), attach proof if you have it, and check back once they've looked into it.
